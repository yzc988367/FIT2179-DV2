Where Australia Is Ageing

Older Australians are not evenly spread across the country. This story explores how population ageing differs between cities, coastal regions, rural inland areas and remote communities.

1. Australia Is Getting Older

Australia's population is ageing steadily. Since 2001, the share of Australians aged 65 and over has risen from 12.6% to 17.3% — an increase of nearly five percentage points in just over two decades. The number of older Australians has nearly doubled.

The fastest growth is in the 85+ age group, which has more than doubled since 2001. All three older age groups are growing, but the oldest Australians are increasing most rapidly as life expectancy improves. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

2. Where Older Australians Live

Ageing is not uniform across the country. Some local government areas have fewer than 10% of residents aged 65+, while others exceed 45%. The two maps below tell different but complementary stories — one shows the proportion of older residents in each LGA, the other shows where the largest numbers of older Australians are concentrated.

Coastal and regional LGAs — particularly along the NSW and Victorian coasts — show the highest proportions of older residents, while remote inland areas and outer-suburban growth corridors tend to have younger age profiles. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024; ABS ASGS Edition 3 Digital Boundary Files.

The largest absolute numbers of older Australians are concentrated in capital cities and major coastal centres. While some regional LGAs have high percentages of older residents, the greatest numbers are in metropolitan areas. Circle size is proportional to the number of people aged 65+. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

3. Cities, Regions and Remote Areas Differ

The experience of ageing differs sharply depending on where you live. Some regional coastal communities have among the oldest age profiles in the country, while remote areas tend to have younger populations — reflecting different economic opportunities, migration patterns and health outcomes.

Small coastal and rural LGAs dominate the top 20. Many are popular retirement and sea-change destinations where older Australians relocate. Several are in Tasmania, which has the highest state-level proportion of older residents. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

The dashed line marks the national average (17.3%). Tasmania and South Australia lead, while the Northern Territory and Australian Capital Territory have noticeably younger populations. These differences reflect decades of internal migration, economic conditions and fertility patterns. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

Inner regional areas have the highest percentage of older residents (21.8%), while very remote areas have the lowest (10.9%). Major cities sit near the national average. This reflects the outmigration of younger people from regional areas to cities for education and work, while older Australians often remain or relocate to regional coastal communities. Source: AIHW Rural and Remote Health, 2023.

4. Not All Older Populations Look the Same

Looking beyond a single percentage tells a richer story. The age structure within the 65+ population — the balance of 65–74 year-olds, 75–84 year-olds and those 85+ — varies by state and has important implications for aged care, health services and community planning.

In most states, the 65–74 age group (the youngest-old) makes up over half of the 65+ population. South Australia has the highest share of people aged 85+ among its older population. These differences in composition affect the types of services each state needs — from active retirement communities to high-care residential facilities. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

How to read a box plot: Each box represents the range of median ages across LGAs within a state. The line inside the box is the median (the middle LGA). The box shows the middle 50% of LGAs. The whiskers extend to the minimum and maximum values. The wider the box, the more variation there is among LGAs within that state.

Tasmania has the highest median state-level median age and the narrowest spread — meaning most of its LGAs are consistently older. Larger states like Queensland and New South Wales show wide variation, with LGAs ranging from median ages in the low 30s to over 60. Source: ABS Median Age, Sex Ratio and Broad Age Groups, by LGA, 2024.

The oldest-old (85+) are concentrated in a small number of LGAs. These communities face the highest demand for aged care, home support and healthcare services per capita. Several are in regional Victoria and New South Wales. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

5. What This Means

Median age and the proportion of older residents are closely related, but the relationship is not linear — and the variation between LGAs is striking. Each dot below is a local government area. Its position shows where it sits on the ageing spectrum, and its size reflects its total population.

LGAs with median ages above 50 consistently have more than 25% of residents aged 65+. Large metropolitan LGAs (larger bubbles) tend to cluster near the national average, while many small regional LGAs occupy the upper-right corner. Each state has a distinct ageing footprint. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024; ABS Median Age by LGA, 2024.

Three Key Findings

  • 1 Australia is ageing nationally. The proportion of people aged 65+ has risen from 12.6% to 17.3% in 24 years. The 85+ population has more than doubled, bringing growing demand for aged care, healthcare and age-friendly infrastructure.
  • 2 Ageing is geographically uneven. Coastal and regional LGAs have some of the highest proportions of older residents, while remote areas have the lowest. Inner regional areas report 21.8% aged 65+, compared with just 10.9% in very remote communities.
  • 3 Numbers, proportions and age structure tell different stories. Metropolitan areas hold the largest absolute numbers of older Australians, even though their proportion is near the national average. Meanwhile, the mix of younger-old (65–74) and oldest-old (85+) varies significantly by location — and each pattern demands a different policy response.
