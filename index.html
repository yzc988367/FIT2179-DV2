Where Australia Is Ageing

Older Australians are not evenly spread across the country. This story shows how ageing differs between cities, regions and remote communities.

1. Australia Is Getting Older

Australia's older population has grown steadily since 2001.

Australia's population is ageing steadily. Since 2001, the share of Australians aged 65 and over has risen from 12.6% to 17.3%, while the number of older Australians has nearly doubled. This national trend sets the scene for the local and regional differences explored below.

The proportion of Australians aged 65+ has risen every year since 2001. The pace has accelerated since 2011, when the first Baby Boomer cohort reached age 65. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

The fastest growth is in the 85+ age group, which has more than doubled since 2001. All three older age groups are growing, but the oldest Australians are increasing most rapidly as life expectancy improves. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

2. Where Older Australians Live

Ageing is geographically uneven across local areas.

Ageing is not spread evenly across Australia. Some local government areas have fewer than 10% of residents aged 65+, while others exceed 45%. The choropleth map shows the proportion of older residents in each LGA, while the proportional symbol map shows the absolute number of older residents. Together, they separate places with high ageing shares from places with large older populations.

Coastal and regional LGAs – particularly along the NSW and Victorian coasts – show some of the highest proportions of older residents. Blue areas are below the national average of 17.3%, while orange areas are above it. Remote inland areas and some outer-suburban growth corridors tend to have younger age profiles. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024; ABS ASGS Edition 3 Digital Boundary Files.

This second map switches from percentages to counts. Large circles show where the largest numbers of older Australians live.

The largest absolute numbers of older Australians are concentrated in capital cities and major coastal centres. While some regional LGAs have high percentages of older residents, the greatest numbers are in metropolitan areas. Circle size is proportional to the number of people aged 65+. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

3. Cities, Regions and Remote Areas Differ

Regional and state patterns show different ageing profiles.

The local patterns in the maps also appear at broader regional and state levels. Some coastal, rural and regional areas have older age profiles, while very remote areas tend to have younger populations. This section compares LGA rankings, state and territory patterns, and remoteness categories to show that ageing varies by place type.

Small coastal and rural LGAs dominate the top rankings. Queenscliffe and Victor Harbor stand out, which is consistent with the older age profiles often seen in retirement and sea-change communities. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

The dashed line marks the national average (17.3%). Tasmania and South Australia lead, while the Northern Territory and Australian Capital Territory have noticeably younger populations. These differences reflect decades of internal migration, economic conditions and fertility patterns. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

Inner regional areas have the highest percentage of older residents (21.8%), while very remote areas have the lowest (10.9%). Major cities sit near the national average. This reflects the outmigration of younger people from regional areas to cities for education and work, while older Australians often remain or relocate to regional coastal communities. Source: AIHW Rural and Remote Health, 2023.

4. Not All Older Populations Look the Same

The 65+ population includes very different age groups.

The 65+ population is not one single group. People aged 65–74, 75–84 and 85+ may represent different stages of later life, and the balance between these groups varies across states and local areas. This helps show why local planning needs to consider not only how many older residents there are, but also which older age groups are growing.

Every state and territory has older residents across the 65–74, 75–84 and 85+ groups, but the balance between these groups differs. This matters because each age band can be linked to different levels of independence, care and community support needs. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

This chart shows variation in median age across LGAs within each state and territory. Tasmania and South Australia generally have older LGA profiles, while larger states such as Queensland, New South Wales and Victoria show wider local variation. Source: ABS Median Age, Sex Ratio and Broad Age Groups by LGA, 2024. ACT appears as a thin line because it has very little LGA-level variation in this dataset.

The oldest-old group is concentrated in a small number of local areas. A higher 85+ share may indicate stronger local need for age-friendly housing, community support and aged care planning. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024.

5. What This Means

Numbers, proportions, and older-age structure tell different stories.

The final section brings the story together. Percentages and population counts can tell different stories: some small areas have high older-population shares, while larger population centres may contain many older residents even if their percentage is lower. Together, the charts show why ageing should be understood as both a national trend and a local planning issue.

Each dot is an LGA. Further right means a larger total population; higher up means a higher share aged 65+. This helps show why both percentages and absolute numbers matter. Source: ABS Regional Population by Age and Sex, 2024; ABS Median Age by LGA, 2024.

Three Key Findings

  • 1 Australia is ageing nationally. The proportion of people aged 65+ has risen from 12.6% to 17.3% since 2001. The 85+ population has more than doubled, adding pressure for aged care, healthcare and age-friendly infrastructure.
  • 2 Ageing is geographically uneven. Coastal and regional LGAs have some of the highest proportions of older residents, while very remote areas are younger in this dataset. Local planning needs will therefore differ sharply between communities.
  • 3 Numbers, proportions and age structure tell different stories. Metropolitan areas can contain many older residents even when their ageing share is moderate. The mix of 65–74, 75–84 and 85+ residents also affects the type of support each place may need.
